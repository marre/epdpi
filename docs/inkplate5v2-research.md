# ED052TC4 on Inkplate 5 Gen 2: timing, drive architecture, and whether `epdpi` is the right foundation

> **Archived Deep Research report — 2026-11-24.** This document preserves the research conclusions and its comparisons of ED052TC4, Soldered, EPDiy, FastEPD and `epdpi`. It is **not** the current implementation task list. For the subsequent decision to retain `esp-hal` unless concrete failures justify changing DMA, and for a no-instrument bring-up plan, see [the implementation plan](inkplate5v2-implementation-plan.md). The report's original preference for deterministic, pre-armed DMA and laboratory validation remains recorded below. Citations from the original research interface are presented as a source list at the end.


## Executive conclusion

The most important conclusion is that **you do not need to reproduce every microsecond delay in Soldered’s implementation exactly, but you do need to reproduce the electrical protocol at a substantially lower level than “roughly the same delays.”** The ED052TC4 datasheet gives fairly generous limits for several gate-control timings, but it gives much tighter constraints around the source-driver start signal `XSTL`/SPH, the XCL/data relationship, latch timing, rail sequencing, and VCOM. Soldered’s apparently arbitrary 7/10/8/10/18/18 µs delays are therefore best treated as a known-working *initial recipe*, not as the panel specification. The main architectural issue in the current Rust port is consequently **not whether a `delay_us(10)` sometimes takes 11 or 14 µs**. It is that Soldered arms the I2S DMA descriptor/link before asserting the source-start condition and then starts transmission immediately, whereas the current `esp-hal` path can perform peripheral/DMA setup after SPH has already gone active. Your own bring-up notes correctly identify this as a remaining discrepancy. The ED052TC4 datasheet places an upper bound on XSTL hold relative to XCL that, at Soldered’s approximate 16 MHz source clock, is only about 15 µs. That makes “HAL work while SPH is low” a genuine candidate for a protocol violation, rather than merely a cosmetic timing mismatch. My recommendation is therefore **keep `epdpi`, but change the transport abstraction rather than trying to force ED052TC4 through the existing generic transaction semantics unchanged**. A small classic-ESP32-specific I2S backend that can pre-arm DMA and expose deterministic “prepared row → assert SPH/CKV → start TX → hardware EOF → deassert SPH” semantics is a better engineering trade than either abandoning `epdpi` or attempting to reproduce Soldered timings above a generic HAL API that does not expose the necessary boundary. `epdpi`’s separation between panel, waveform and transport is useful; the transport contract is the piece that needs strengthening. For waveform behavior, **Soldered remains the strongest ED052TC4-specific executable reference**. The panel itself is specified as capable of 2–16 gray levels depending on controller and waveform, but Soldered exposes eight levels and uses a nine-phase 3-bit waveform. EPDiy has broader and more sophisticated differential/waveform machinery, but its ED052TC4 entry is still labelled “yes (should work),” rather than “tested,” and its ED052 support had a resolution-definition defect corrected only in 2026. FastEPD is particularly worth studying because it explicitly supports the Inkplate 5 Gen 2 and has evolved features such as 2-bpp grayscale and differential updates, but it is better viewed as a source of architecture and optimization ideas than as a reason to replace your Rust core. Most importantly, **do not remove the current high-voltage interlock until the low-voltage protocol has been verified on actual pins and the PMIC sequencing has been scoped**. CI can establish software invariants; it cannot establish source-clock polarity/order, actual XSTL pulse duration, latch width, VCOM value, rail rise/fall order, or whether the display sees any unintended clocks while rails collapse. Your current diagnostic-first approach is the correct safety boundary.

## What the actual ED052TC4 specification says

The useful document is E Ink technical specification **P-511(A77)-003(V:2), revision 2.0, dated November 30, 2017**. Revision 2.0 explicitly changed the mechanical drawing and electrical-characteristics page. It describes ED052TC4 as a 5.17-inch Carta electrophoretic panel with a native geometry of **720 horizontal × 1280 vertical pixels**. It also states that the module can support **2–16 gray levels, depending on the display controller and associated waveform file**. That last qualification is important: “16 gray” is a panel capability, not a promise that any arbitrary direct-drive waveform will produce 16 reliable, calibrated levels. The 50-pin panel interface is not a controller-style SPI interface; the MCU/board is effectively acting as the timing controller. Relevant signals include CKV for the gate scan, SPV for vertical start, XCL for source shifting, XLE for source latch, XSTL for source start, D0–D7 for parallel pixel drive data, and the panel’s high-voltage source/gate rails. The connector specified by the panel document is WP27D-P050VA3, which is also the connector EPDiy lists for its ED052TC4 support. Electrically, the panel expects approximately **+15 V VPOS, −15 V VNEG, +22 V VGH, and −20 V VGL** in addition to its low-voltage logic supply. The document gives adjusted VCOM rather than a universal fixed value and recommends using the assigned VCOM within ±0.1 V. It also gives a substantial common-electrode inrush figure, approximately 600 mA, which is another reason not to regard PMIC operation as a mere Boolean “power on.” The guaranteed normal operating temperature range in the electrical section is 0–50 °C. The digital timing numbers are more revealing than Soldered’s source-code sleeps:

| Signal/timing | ED052TC4 specification | Engineering implication |
|---|---:|---|
| CKV frequency | ≤ 200 kHz | Row/gate scan must not outrun the gate driver. |
| CKV high / low time | ≥ 0.5 µs each | Exact 7–18 µs Soldered delays are not panel minima. |
| SPV setup / hold | ≥ 100 ns | Expander/I²C latency makes microsecond-exact matching unnecessary here, provided ordering is correct. |
| XCL cycle | ≥ 16.67 ns; 50 ns typical | Source interface permits up to ~60 MHz by timing minimum; Soldered runs much slower. |
| D0–D7 setup / hold | ≥ 8 ns | Actual wire ordering and clock edge matter. |
| XSTL setup | 0.5–0.8 × XCL cycle | This is a *tight* relationship to the source clock. |
| XSTL hold | ≥0.5 × cycle and ≤ roughly 240 cycles minus setup | There is a real maximum; an arbitrarily delayed DMA start can violate it. |
| XLE turn-on delay | ≥3.5 XCL cycles | Latch placement is protocol, not merely software sequencing. |
| XLE high | ≥300 ns under the stated condition | A back-to-back GPIO set/clear deserves measurement. |
| XLE off delay | ≥200 ns | Next operation cannot simply be assumed safe from source-code order. |
| Source output settling | ≤20 µs under specified load | Row-to-row timing should be verified at the panel, not inferred from API return times. |

These values come from the panel’s AC-characteristics table and timing diagrams. The datasheet additionally says the first active gate line appears only after the gate-start sequence has progressed through five CKV clocks. This distinction answers the central timing question. **The panel does not care that an Arduino source file contains the literal integer `10`; it cares that XSTL, XCL, data, XLE, CKV and SPV arrive in a legal electrical relationship.** Some of the Soldered delays are comfortably larger than panel minima and therefore probably provide conservative margin. Other properties—especially XSTL-to-XCL and source-data clocking—are fundamentally edge-relative and cannot safely be represented as “approximately the same microseconds.” The power sequence is similarly specific. On power-up, the source rails are ordered **VSS → VDD → VNEG → VPOS → VCOM**, while the gate path is **VSS → VDD → VGL → VGH**. The table includes minimum separations such as 1 ms between VNEG and the following source-rail stage and 1 ms on the gate sequence. On shutdown, the panel requires controlled decay and specifically calls for at least 0.5 s at the stated −7.4 V discharge point; the notes require VGL to stay sufficiently negative relative to VCOM during the decay. The datasheet lists an **85 Hz maximum refresh rate**, not a target frequency. The optical/electrical characterizations also carry a strong qualification: their guaranteed behavior is tied to E Ink’s controller and waveform conditions. A home-grown direct driver can operate the panel, but its grayscale accuracy, ghosting and long-term DC behavior are no longer automatically covered by those characterization claims.

## What Soldered actually does, and which parts matter

Soldered’s Inkplate 5 Gen 2 implementation is considerably more than an image buffer plus an I2S transfer. It allocates a DMA row of **320 payload bytes plus 16 extra bytes**, or 336 parallel samples per display row, initializes classic ESP32 I2S1 in LCD/parallel mode, and sets `tx_right_first = 0`. The source implementation prepares/reset the DMA machinery, assigns and starts the out-link, drives SPH low and CKV high, then asserts `tx_start`; it busy-waits on `out_total_eof`, raises SPH immediately when EOF is observed, and only then stops/cleans up the DMA link. That ordering is one of the most important details in the entire reference implementation. **DMA setup occurs before the panel is told that a source row is starting.** By contrast, your current bring-up document records that the `esp-hal` path still performs HAL transfer setup after SPH/CKV have entered the row-active state and raises SPH only after HAL completion/resource-return work. Your documentation also correctly notes that matching the packed bytes in RAM does not prove matching the physical byte sequence because Soldered and the HAL use different `tx_right_first` settings. At an approximate 16 MHz XCL, one cycle is 62.5 ns. Applying the datasheet’s XSTL maximum of roughly 240 source-clock cycles gives about **15 µs of permissible XSTL hold**, depending on exact setup position. That is a derived limit, not a measured failure threshold, but it puts the current HAL issue into perspective: a few microseconds of jitter inside a 10 µs gate delay is probably benign; an API that leaves SPH asserted while it configures DMA for an unbounded number of microseconds may not be. The vertical-start sequence in Soldered uses waits of approximately **7, 10, 8, 10, 18 and 18 µs** interleaved with CKV/SPV transitions. Those numbers are all far above the datasheet’s 100 ns SPV and 0.5 µs CKV minima, so there is no evidence in the public panel specification that *those exact numeric values* are required. Their safer interpretation is “a known-working gate-start recipe, with plenty of digital timing margin.” The datasheet’s “first gate after five CKV clocks” relationship is the underlying invariant that deserves attention. There is an important subtlety in your use of the PCAL6416A for SPV. Because SPV changes traverse I²C, a source-level “delay 7 µs after SPV” is not equivalent to a pin-to-pin 7 µs interval; the transaction itself adds variable time. Soldered lives with the same fundamental expander overhead. That strongly argues that the 7/10/18 µs values should be interpreted as minimum software gaps after state changes, not nanosecond-calibrated waveform parameters. Your bring-up notes already call this out. The 16 additional I2S samples per row are not documented in the public ED052TC4 specification as a formal “16 dummy clocks” requirement. They are nevertheless present in Soldered’s working implementation and therefore should be retained during initial validation. Removing or changing them before you have XCL/XSTL/XLE captures would be optimizing an unexplained but proven margin. Using Soldered’s stated roughly 16 MHz I2S clock as the working assumption, 336 parallel byte samples take about **21.0 µs per row**; the 320 active samples occupy about 20.0 µs and the 16 trailing samples about 1.0 µs. A 720-row scan therefore has a theoretical wire time of about **15.12 ms**. Adding the explicitly programmed ~63 µs vertical-start waits and 230 µs inter-frame delay gives a lower bound near **15.4 ms per scan**, or about 65 scans/s, before row software overhead and I²C transactions. This is comfortably below the panel’s 85 Hz maximum. These are derived estimates and should not be treated as measured Inkplate timings until XCL is scoped. Grayscale is also instructive. Soldered markets and documents the Inkplate 5V2 as **3-bit, eight-level grayscale**, despite the ED052TC4 panel specification allowing up to 16 levels with an appropriate controller/waveform. Its driver contains an eight-entry, nine-phase drive matrix and performs a substantial conditioning/cleaning sequence before the nine grayscale phases. The result is therefore not simply “nine frames = one grayscale update”; the full waveform contains many more complete scans. For the current Soldered 3-bit path, the preconditioning sequence amounts to 48 full scans before the nine grayscale scans, followed by another cleaning scan: roughly **58 full panel scans** in the complete operation. At the ~15.4 ms lower-bound scan duration above, that alone implies roughly **0.89 s minimum**, before CPU packing and PMIC overhead. Soldered’s published full-refresh figure is 0.99 s, which is remarkably consistent with that calculation and strongly suggests the ~16 MHz/336-sample interpretation is in the right regime. That agreement is useful because it also shows where efficiency gains are possible. Making the individual row transfer a few percent faster will not transform a one-second grayscale refresh: the dominant cost is the number of full-panel waveform scans. Meaningful optimization later will come from better differential/partial waveforms, fewer conditioning phases where safe, and shorter HV-on duration—not from shaving one microsecond from `vscan_start`.

## Other implementations of ED052TC4 and Inkplate 5 Gen 2

There are now more useful comparison points than Soldered alone.

**EPDiy** explicitly lists ED052TC4, 1280×720, WP27D-P050VA3, but its current compatibility table still says **“yes (should work)”**, whereas panels with stronger validation are labelled “yes, tested.” ED052TC4 support was first discussed in November 2024, and an adapter was subsequently developed for it. This makes EPDiy relevant engineering evidence, but not a better ED052TC4-specific golden reference than the actual Inkplate firmware. There is another reason to qualify EPDiy’s ED052 maturity: its ED052 display metadata retained an incorrect height until an issue and corrective PR in 2026. That does not undermine EPDiy as a project; it does mean “EPDiy supports ED052TC4” should not be read as “EPDiy’s ED052 implementation is more exhaustively validated than Soldered’s.” Where EPDiy is substantially ahead is **waveform architecture**. Its high-level driver models transitions from old gray to new gray rather than merely converting a target framebuffer into a fixed series of pulses. It supports waveform modes, temperature selection, partial areas and differential updates. Its documentation also makes explicit that waveform phase timing can affect electrophoretic movement: on older ESP32/I2S EPDiy hardware, CKV high time was varied experimentally to alter how long a row was driven, while the ESP32-S3/LCD-peripheral generation generally assumes fixed frame timing and encodes behavior through repeated phases instead. That is a useful architectural lesson for `epdpi`: **row timing and electrophoretic waveform timing are conceptually separate things**. The raw panel transport should establish a repeatable row/frame duration; the waveform layer should decide what drive state each old→new gray transition receives during each phase. Mixing “faster grayscale” into ad-hoc CKV timing makes both electrical validation and waveform reasoning harder. **FastEPD** is particularly relevant because its current README explicitly names **Inkplate 5 Gen 2** as a supported target rather than merely listing ED052TC4 as theoretically compatible. FastEPD was created specifically as an alternative to the growing complexity of EPDiy and uses DMA-based panel output with selectable update strategies. Its 2026 releases added 2-bpp grayscale, non-flashing differential updates between bit depths, alternative clearing strategies and warnings about charge imbalance when updates repeatedly push in one electrophoretic direction. That FastEPD warning is worth taking seriously. E-paper waveform quality is not only a visual concern; **DC/charge balance is part of panel health and repeatability**. An update mode that looks faster can still be undesirable if repeated use causes accumulated bias, graying or ghosting. This is precisely why the Soldered cleaning sequence should initially be copied faithfully and only optimized after repeated-pattern testing. There is also a newer **ESP-IDF Inkplate port** that added Inkplate 5V2 support in March 2026 and reorganized its I2S communication and E-Ink power setup. It describes itself as prerelease software, so it is useful as another implementation to inspect rather than an authoritative timing source. Separately, Soldered’s ESPHome integration now lists Inkplate 5 V2 with eight-level grayscale and partial black/white updates, again demonstrating that the board can be supported outside the original Arduino stack without changing the fundamental panel-drive model. The practical landscape is therefore:

| Project | Exact Inkplate 5 Gen 2 / ED052 relevance | Strengths | Weaknesses for your use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Soldered Arduino** | Exact board and panel; shipping reference | Proven pinout, PMIC, I2S, 3-bit waveform, partial BW | Architecture is board-specific; busy-waiting; limited waveform abstraction |
| **Soldered ESPHome** | Exact board supported | Another independent integration; BW partial + 8-gray support | Still substantially derived from Inkplate behavior |
| **ESP-IDF-InkPlate** | Exact Inkplate 5V2 support added in 2026 | Native IDF, useful I2S/power comparison | Declared prerelease |
| **FastEPD** | Explicit Inkplate 5 Gen 2 target | Optimized DMA, several bit depths, differential/partial ideas, active development | C/C++; generic update modes are not necessarily ED052 vendor-qualified |
| **EPDiy** | ED052TC4 connector/display listed | Most mature general direct-drive waveform architecture; temperature/differential concepts | ED052 listed “should work,” not “tested”; separate board/PMIC architecture |
| **`epdpi`** | Your ED052 work is board-specific but native Rust | `no_std`, clean panel/transport separation, small conceptual surface | Transport abstraction currently hides a timing boundary that ED052 exposes |

This evidence does **not** support a wholesale pivot to EPDiy. It supports borrowing the mature concepts EPDiy and FastEPD have accumulated while preserving `epdpi` as the Rust-facing core.

## Is `epdpi` the right foundation?

For this specific product, **yes—with one important architectural correction**.

`epdpi`’s model is fundamentally sensible for a Rust library: panel geometry and waveform logic are distinct from a hardware-specific parallel bus; the framebuffer can remain independent of ESP32 transport; and the chip-specific implementation resides in `epdpi-esp`. That is exactly the separation you want if the application code is going to remain Rust and the board is fixed. The mistake would be assuming that a generic parallel-bus trait only needs operations like “send row bytes” and can let the underlying HAL choose when the wire transaction actually begins. For ED052TC4, the transport boundary should describe **wire-visible state**, not API-visible state. A good internal contract is something like: prepare a row buffer and DMA descriptor while the panel is idle; arm/reset the peripheral; assert source-start/gate signals; issue a single low-latency “go” operation; wait on the actual hardware EOF condition; immediately restore SPH/CKV; then perform non-time-critical resource cleanup. That is almost exactly what the Soldered register-level implementation does. I would not let `esp-hal` ownership semantics dictate the electrical protocol. If its stable abstraction cannot preconfigure the classic ESP32 I2S link before SPH is lowered and cannot expose a completion point sufficiently close to `out_total_eof`, implement a **small, narrowly audited register-level classic-ESP32 transport in Rust**. This is not a rejection of `esp-hal`; it is the normal place for a board driver to drop beneath a HAL when peripheral sequencing itself is part of the external protocol. Recent unrelated work on classic ESP32 I2S parallel output has likewise shown that FIFO mode, `tx_right_first`, register-write ordering and GPIO-matrix routing can all determine what actually appears on the wire despite software transfers appearing to succeed. That backend should remain behind the same higher-level `epdpi` interface so you do not infect the waveform and drawing layers with ESP32 register knowledge. In other words, **refactor downward, not outward**.

I would also retain the present 4-bpp framebuffer representation. At 1280×720, one Gray4 framebuffer is **460,800 bytes**; two are 921,600 bytes. That is expensive but quite reasonable on a WROVER with PSRAM, and it gives cheap nibble addressing plus compatibility with rendering APIs. Packing exactly three bits per pixel would reduce one frame to 345,600 bytes but save only 115,200 bytes per frame at the cost of much more expensive random pixel access and packing. Soldered itself exposes only eight gray values, but keeping the logical framebuffer at 4 bpp leaves room for dithering or a future 16-level waveform without changing the graphics API. Soldered’s ESPHome support also explicitly requires PSRAM for these large parallel-board framebuffers. Two full framebuffers should, however, be **optional by update mode**. Differential/partial updating genuinely needs an old-state representation; a one-shot full grayscale render does not inherently need two 4-bpp screens. That is a better memory optimization than changing the public pixel encoding to a densely packed 3-bpp format. EPDiy and FastEPD’s old→new transition architectures illustrate why the second buffer becomes valuable once sophisticated differential modes are introduced. On CPU efficiency, do not optimize for low CPU utilization during the first successful display refresh. Soldered polls DMA EOF synchronously, so DMA offloads the high-frequency parallel clock/data generation but the CPU remains occupied around every row. An interrupt-driven row-completion scheme could save CPU cycles, but it also inserts interrupt latency exactly where SPH needs prompt deassertion. On a slowly refreshed e-paper display, spending a core for roughly a second during a full refresh is generally a much better trade than making the row boundary nondeterministic. Where the architecture should eventually grow is the **waveform layer**. `epdpi` should be able to express: current gray → target gray transition tables; a sequence of phases; optionally temperature-selected waveform sets; full versus partial modes; and explicit neutral/positive/negative drive states. EPDiy’s model is the strongest reference for that abstraction, while FastEPD provides useful recent examples of lower-bit-depth and differential modes.

## High-voltage, VCOM, grayscale, and long-term panel behavior

The PMIC deserves independent validation from the row timing. ED052TC4 does not merely need “high voltage present”; its positive and negative source and gate rails have specified nominal ranges and sequencing, and VCOM is panel-specific. The TPS65186/Inkplate circuitry is intended to provide those functions, so the safest Rust implementation is to reproduce the board vendor’s proven PMIC use rather than invent a generic rail controller. Your current power code is conservative in a good way: it checks the PMIC power-good status, bounds waits and does not rewrite VCOM EEPROM, while actual HV enable remains blocked pending hardware validation. That is preferable to “improving” VCOM handling before the value programmed for the physical panel has been read and compared with the panel’s assigned value. The ED052 datasheet explicitly requires the assigned VCOM to be respected to within ±0.1 V. Do not use retailer temperature ranges or absolute maxima as waveform operating limits. The ED052TC4 electrical specification’s guaranteed operating range is 0–50 °C; some third-party listings conflate broader absolute/storage limits with operation. Any future temperature compensation should therefore be based on actual ED052 waveform data or controlled empirical characterization, not a generic Carta lookup table copied from another panel. The panel’s “16 gray” claim is similarly easy to overread. Soldered provides eight levels because its waveform is a 3-bit/eight-state waveform. EPDiy’s support for 16-level rendering on many panels demonstrates that richer transitions are possible in this class of hardware, but there is no public ED052TC4 vendor waveform in the sources I found that would justify replacing the Soldered eight-level table with an invented 16-level one. Until such a waveform is obtained or characterized, **eight reliable levels are a better product feature than sixteen nominal ones with unknown DC balance and poor level spacing**. Likewise, Soldered’s nine grayscale phases should not be interpreted as a universal ED052 waveform supplied by E Ink. They are a board-vendor implementation that demonstrably works. The public E Ink specification does not include the proprietary electrophoretic transition LUT necessary to reproduce qualified 16-gray behavior. This is one of the important missing specifications in the public material. Partial refresh is a separate question. Soldered’s implementation limits partial updating to black/white transitions, and its public product/software documentation describes partial updates alongside eight-level full grayscale. That is a sensible first milestone for Rust too: establish correct full 3-bit grayscale and proven BW differential partial refresh before experimenting with partial grayscale. Power-off pin state matters as much as power-on sequencing. Soldered ultimately quiesces the clock path and tri-states relevant panel pins; your bring-up document records that the Rust implementation does not yet exactly duplicate that behavior. Because the ED052 datasheet imposes rail-decay ordering, an unintended XCL/CKV/data transition while one rail is collapsing is not something software-unit tests can rule out. The shutdown state therefore belongs in the hardware validation checklist, not in a later “power optimization” milestone.

## Recommended implementation and validation path

The evidence points to a relatively narrow set of changes rather than a rewrite. In priority order:

1. **Keep the HV lock in place and first validate the low-voltage bus with a logic analyzer.** Capture XCL, one or more D pins, SPH/XSTL, CKV, LE/XLE and, if practical, SPV. Compare Soldered and Rust on the same board or equivalent boards. Confirm XCL frequency/polarity, actual D0–D7 byte/lane order, number of XCL edges per row, SPH-to-first-XCL and last-XCL-to-SPH intervals, CKV state during the row, LE width and the 16 trailing samples. This directly resolves the `tx_right_first` uncertainty that host-side packed-byte tests cannot answer. 2. **Change the classic-ESP32 transport so DMA is fully prepared before SPH is asserted.** Ideally the only operation between SPH/CKV row-start and I2S transmission is a register-level `tx_start`; use the hardware `out_total_eof` condition as the timing boundary for SPH deassertion. If `esp-hal` cannot guarantee this, add a small target-specific backend rather than contorting the panel layer. 3. **Do not chase exact Soldered microsecond constants yet.** Preserve the 7/10/8/10/18/18 µs sequence for the first physical refresh, because it is known-working, but judge success against datasheet bounds and measured pins. The important exception is any timing coupled to XCL—particularly XSTL and XLE—which should be checked in nanoseconds/source-clock cycles rather than from Rust delay calls. 4. **Scope the PMIC before enabling a panel waveform.** Verify VNEG, VPOS, VGL, VGH and VCOM values and startup order; verify PWR_GOOD behavior; then capture shutdown and confirm controlled discharge and lack of unintended clock activity. Do not alter the stored VCOM merely to make the software path look complete. 5. **Bring up the simplest DC-balanced pattern before grayscale.** Use alternating known drive codes and full-white/full-black conditioning patterns to verify spatial orientation, nibble/lane order and that all 720×1280 pixels are addressed. Then reproduce Soldered’s complete cleaning sequence and nine-phase eight-gray waveform exactly. Only after that should waveform cleanup or speed optimization begin. 6. **Measure waveform quality, not merely whether an image appears.** Use repeated black↔white, mid-gray ramps, checkerboards and partial-update stress patterns. Record full-update duration, ghost residual, gray monotonicity and behavior after tens/hundreds of repeated updates. FastEPD’s explicit warning about charge imbalance is a good reminder that an apparently successful short waveform is not necessarily a safe long-term waveform. 7. **After first stable release, evolve `epdpi` toward transition waveforms rather than replacing it.** Borrow EPDiy’s old-gray→new-gray and temperature/mode abstractions and FastEPD’s differential/bit-depth ideas, while keeping the Rust no-std panel/transport separation. Add a second framebuffer only when the selected mode needs prior-state information. 8. **Optimize rail-on time last.** Your theoretical ~0.9 s Soldered grayscale sequence already explains almost all of the published ~0.99 s full-refresh time. The largest future efficiency gains will come from reducing the number of waveform scans for common transitions, especially differential and partial modes, rather than increasing XCL or shortening the vertical-start pauses. Any reduced waveform needs ghosting/DC-balance qualification before becoming the default.

## Bottom line

The ED052TC4 is forgiving about several **gate-control delays** but not forgiving about **source-driver edge relationships, byte ordering, latch timing, rail sequencing and waveform/DC balance**. That is why attempting to reproduce every Soldered `delayMicroseconds()` call exactly is both unnecessary and, in places, the wrong level of abstraction. The highest-risk discrepancy in the current port is the one already identified in your bring-up notes: **the HAL starts doing transfer setup after SPH has gone active**. Fix that before spending effort tuning the 7/10/18 µs constants. With an XCL near 16 MHz, the panel’s XSTL maximum translates to a source-start window of only roughly 15 µs, so the ordering difference is capable of being electrically material. `epdpi` was therefore **a reasonable starting choice and remains the one I would use**, but I would not insist on using its current lowest-level transport abstraction unchanged. Keep the Rust panel/waveform/framebuffer model; add an ED052-capable prepared-DMA transport primitive and, if necessary, a small register-level classic-ESP32 backend. This gives you the determinism of Soldered’s implementation without inheriting its whole C++ architecture. Use **Soldered as the ED052/Inkplate electrical oracle, the E Ink datasheet as the legal envelope, EPDiy as the waveform-architecture reference, and FastEPD as the optimization/differential-update reference**. None of the alternatives currently supplies enough ED052TC4-specific evidence to justify a wholesale pivot, while all of them contain ideas worth importing. EPDiy still labels ED052TC4 as “should work”; FastEPD explicitly supports Inkplate 5 Gen 2 but uses its own generic update architecture; and Soldered remains the only implementation in this comparison whose exact board, PMIC, expander and shipped ED052 panel combination matches your target. The public information still leaves three important unknowns: **the genuine E Ink ED052TC4 waveform file is not public in the material located; the origin/rationale for Soldered’s 16 trailing source clocks is not documented; and source-code inspection cannot prove actual classic-ESP32 I2S wire ordering or pulse widths.** Those are measurement problems, not more-software-analysis problems. A logic-analyzer capture of Soldered versus the Rust backend, followed by a scope capture of the PMIC rails, will resolve more remaining risk than further attempts to make the Rust delay constants visually match the C++ source.
